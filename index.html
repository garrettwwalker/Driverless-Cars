Wasted time

A full work week, gone, every year.

The typical U.S. driver lost 49 hours to congestion in 2025 — worth about $894 in lost time each, and $85 billion nationally. Autonomous fleets don't repeal traffic, but smoother merging, no circling for parking, and higher road throughput are among the reasons cities model congestion falling rather than rising.

Reclaimed space

We paved a second country for parked cars.

Researchers put the U.S. parking supply somewhere between 700 million and 2 billion spaces — 2.5 to 7 for every vehicle. In some North American cities, parking alone covers about 14% of incorporated land before a single road is counted; downtown Los Angeles is closer to 24%. A shared autonomous fleet needs a fraction of it — cars that don't sit still 95% of the day don't need somewhere to sit.

The Waymo record

The safety case is no longer hypothetical.

As of March 2026, Waymo reported more than 170 million fully autonomous miles — about 200 human lifetimes of driving — and now covers roughly 4 million miles a week. Its comparison is against human-driver crash rates in the same cities.

Method & sources

How the numbers are built.

The preventable-deaths model

For each year from your chosen start year to now, the model computes:

preventable(year) = recorded_deaths(year) × autonomous_share(year) × crash_reduction

  • recorded_deaths — NHTSA FARS final counts for 2014–2023; NHTSA early estimates for 2024 (39,345) and 2025 (≈36,640). The current year is prorated by the fraction elapsed.
  • autonomous_share — a straight-line ramp from 0 at your start year to 50% after the number of years you set, continuing at the same slope and capped at 95%. Real adoption would not be a straight line.
  • crash_reduction — the slider you set, defaulting to 85%. Waymo's own figure for serious-or-fatal-injury crashes is 92%; an IIHS analysis implies roughly 68% fewer crashes per mile.
